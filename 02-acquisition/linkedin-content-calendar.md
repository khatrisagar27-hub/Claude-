## POST 1 — Monday, Week 1
**Topic: Section 2(22)(e) — The Deemed Dividend Trap**

---

⚠️ A promoter took ₹50 lakh as a "loan" from his own company.

He planned to repay it. It was temporary. It was his company.

The Income Tax Department treated it as dividend. He paid tax on ₹50 lakh he thought was a loan.

This is Section 2(22)(e) — Deemed Dividend.

📌 How it works:

If a private limited company gives a loan or advance to —
✅ A shareholder holding 10%+ voting power, OR
✅ A concern where such shareholder holds 20%+ beneficial interest

— the loan amount is treated as dividend in the shareholder's hands, to the extent of the company's accumulated profits.

❌ The tax is not on the company. It's on the promoter personally.
❌ The loan being repaid later doesn't undo the tax liability.
❌ It is one of the most common additions in scrutiny of closely held companies.

💡 Promoters who route personal expenses, drawings, or temporary cash needs through the company account — often without realising the tax character — are sitting on this exposure.

If your company has outstanding loans to directors or related entities in the books, this provision deserves a review before the next assessment cycle.

## POST 2 — Thursday, Week 1
**Topic: Section 80JJAA — A Deduction Most Manufacturing Businesses Never Claim**

---

🏭 Most manufacturing businesses expanding their workforce are leaving a significant deduction unclaimed.

Section 80JJAA provides an ADDITIONAL 30% deduction on wages paid to new employees — over and above the salary expense already claimed.

📌 Who qualifies:

✅ Business subject to tax audit under Section 44AB
✅ New employee hired during the year (above the opening headcount)
✅ Employee worked at least 240 days in the year
✅ Monthly emoluments not exceeding ₹25,000
✅ Payment made through banking channels

📊 Real numbers:

50 new workers hired at ₹15,000/month:

Salary expense already deducted → ₹90 lakh
Additional 80JJAA deduction (30%) → ₹27 lakh EXTRA deduction
Available for 3 consecutive years from the year of hiring

💰 ₹27 lakh additional deduction every year for 3 years — on top of the actual wage cost already claimed.

This deduction is available year after year as headcount grows. It accumulates.

❌ In practice, it is missed in a large proportion of eligible assessments — either because the CA is not tracking new headcount systematically or because payroll data isn't linked to the tax computation.

If your business hired workers this year and you are subject to audit — ask whether 80JJAA has been considered.

## POST 3 — Monday, Week 2
**Topic: Section 50C — The Stamp Duty Value Trap in Property Transactions**

---

🔍 A business owner sold a plot for ₹80 lakh.

The stamp duty circle rate for that area: ₹1.20 crore.

His capital gains were computed on ₹1.20 crore — not on the ₹80 lakh he actually received.

This is Section 50C of the Income Tax Act.

📌 The provision:

Where the actual sale consideration for land or building is LESS than the value adopted by the stamp authority for stamp duty purposes — the stamp duty value is deemed to be the full sale consideration for capital gains computation.

⚠️ The impact runs both ways:

➡️ For the SELLER — Capital gains tax on the higher stamp duty value, even if that amount was never received.

➡️ For the BUYER (individual/HUF) — The difference between stamp duty value and actual price paid may be taxable in the buyer's hands under Section 56(2)(x).

✅ One relief: If stamp duty value does not exceed 110% of actual consideration, the actual price is accepted (Finance Act 2020 amendment).

❌ But if the gap is wider — both parties have a tax exposure on the same difference.

💡 Property transactions within group companies, family settlements, and business restructurings involving immovable assets all carry this risk.

Checking the circle rate before finalising the transaction price — not after signing the agreement — is the only way to manage this correctly.

## POST 4 — Thursday, Week 2
**Topic: GST on Personal Use of Business Assets**

---

📋 A provision in GST that most businesses discover only during a departmental audit:

Schedule I of the CGST Act, 2017 treats certain transactions as SUPPLY — even when made WITHOUT CONSIDERATION.

⚠️ What this means practically:

🔹 Your company availed ITC on business equipment.
🔹 That equipment is later transferred to a related party at below market value — or gifted to an employee.
🔹 GST is payable on the fair market value. The transaction is a supply under Schedule I.

🔹 Your company constructed a building and availed ITC on construction.
🔹 That building is subsequently used for personal / residential purposes.
🔹 The ITC already availed must be reversed under Rule 44 — with interest at 18% if not done voluntarily.

❌ The common mistake:

Businesses assume that transactions without money changing hands have no GST. Schedule I was specifically designed to close this gap.

✅ What requires review:

→ Assets on which ITC was availed and are now being used outside the business
→ Transfers to related parties at below market value
→ Company vehicles used personally where any GST was availed (note: ITC on motor vehicles is blocked under Section 17(5) for most cases — so this doesn't apply to blocked items, but does apply where ITC was legitimately availed)

💡 This comes up in GST audits under Section 65 and departmental scrutiny with increasing regularity. The exposure is ITC reversal + 18% interest + potential penalty.

## POST 5 — Monday, Week 3
**Topic: Section 79 — Ownership Change Kills Carried Forward Losses**

---

⚠️ A company had ₹2 crore of carried forward business losses.

The promoter brought in a new investor — who took a 52% stake.

The ₹2 crore of losses? Gone. Permanently disallowed.

This is Section 79 of the Income Tax Act — and it surprises businesses almost every time.

📌 The provision:

In a closely held company (one in which public are NOT substantially interested) —

If on the last day of the current year, persons holding 51%+ of voting power are DIFFERENT from those who held 51%+ on the last day of the loss year —

→ Carried forward losses from that year CANNOT be set off.

❌ The losses are not suspended or postponed. They are extinguished permanently.

🔍 When this gets triggered:

→ PE or strategic investor acquiring majority stake
→ Family business restructuring where shares pass to the next generation, crossing the 51% threshold
→ Amalgamation or acquisition of a loss-making company
→ Partners diluting below 49% to bring in outside capital

💡 The consequence is direct: the tax benefit of accumulated losses — which could have offset future profits — disappears entirely.

Due diligence on Section 79 implications is a mandatory step in ANY transaction involving ownership change of a company with carried forward losses. It is frequently discovered after the transaction is executed — when it is already too late.

## POST 6 — Thursday, Week 3
**Topic: Section 194N — TDS on Your Own Cash Withdrawals**

---

💰 Your bank deducts TDS when you withdraw your own cash.

Most business owners know this exists. Few know the rate doubles if ITR hasn't been filed.

📌 Section 194N — Current rates:

✅ ITR filed for at least 1 of the last 3 years:
→ 2% TDS on cash withdrawals exceeding ₹1 crore in the financial year

❌ ITR NOT filed for any of the last 3 years:
→ 2% TDS on withdrawals above ₹20 lakh up to ₹1 crore
→ 5% TDS on withdrawals exceeding ₹1 crore

⚠️ The practical problem:

For trading and manufacturing businesses with high cash operations — labour payments, raw material purchases, daily vendor payments — cumulative cash withdrawals from current accounts can cross ₹1 crore mid-year.

If the PAN linked to the account belongs to an entity that hasn't filed ITR for 3 years, the 5% rate applies — and the bank deducts without warning.

📊 On ₹1.5 crore of cash withdrawals:
→ Normal rate (2%): ₹3 lakh TDS
→ Non-filer rate (5% on excess): Substantially higher

✅ The TDS is creditable against your tax liability — it is not lost. But the cash flow impact during the year can be significant.

💡 Regular ITR filing for all entities — the company, the HUF, the proprietorship — and monitoring cumulative cash withdrawal levels across all accounts, is how this is managed correctly.

## POST 7 — Monday, Week 4
**Topic: HUF — What Most People Get Wrong**

---

📋 HUF is well understood as a tax planning tool.

What is less discussed — the boundaries that, when crossed, create problems.

🔹 GIFTS TO HUF FROM NON-MEMBERS

Gifts received by an HUF from its own members are exempt.

Gifts from persons outside the HUF — employers, clients, business associates — are taxable under Section 56(2)(x) if the aggregate exceeds ₹50,000 in a year.

❌ Many HUF accounts receive funds from third parties who are not HUF members. The gifting exemption does not protect this.

---

🔹 PARTIAL PARTITION — NOT RECOGNISED BY INCOME TAX

After 31 December 1978, partial partition of an HUF is not recognised under Section 171 of the Income Tax Act.

Income from assets partially partitioned continues to be assessed as HUF income — even if in reality, those assets are being used individually.

✅ Only a total partition is recognised for tax purposes.

---

🔹 KARTA'S REMUNERATION

An HUF can pay remuneration to the Karta for services rendered to the HUF's business.

✅ It is deductible from HUF income.
✅ It becomes individual income of the Karta — taxed separately.

❌ But: the remuneration must be reasonable, based on actual services, and backed by an HUF resolution. Excessive or undocumented remuneration is disallowed.

---

💡 HUF planning done without understanding these provisions creates tax and estate complications that typically surface years later — during succession, assessment, or property dispute.

## POST 8 — Thursday, Week 4
**Topic: GST RCM on Director Remuneration — Most Companies Are Non-Compliant**

---

⚠️ Companies paying remuneration to Independent or Non-Executive Directors are liable to pay GST under Reverse Charge Mechanism.

Most are not doing it.

📌 CBDT Circular No. 140/10/2020-GST (June 2020) — the position:

✅ Whole-time / Executive Directors:
Remuneration is salary. TDS under Section 192 applies. NO GST.

❌ Independent / Non-Executive Directors:
Remuneration is for services rendered as a director — NOT salary.
GST @ 18% is payable by the COMPANY under RCM.

📋 What the company must do:

→ Issue a self-invoice for the director's fees
→ Pay GST in cash (cannot be offset against ITC balance)
→ Claim ITC on the same GST paid (subject to normal conditions)

⚠️ The consequence of non-compliance:

→ GST demand on the company for all past periods
→ Interest at 18% per annum
→ Penalty up to 100% of tax in cases of non-payment

❌ The common error:

Companies treat ALL director remuneration as salary to avoid this. But if the non-executive director is NOT on payroll, does not receive Form 16, and is paid sitting fees or commission — the salary classification does not hold.

💡 In companies with independent directors, audit committees, or separate advisory board members — each person's classification must be examined individually.

This is one of the most widespread areas of GST non-compliance in mid-size private companies.
